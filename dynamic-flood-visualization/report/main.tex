% 0. ABSTRACT
% ==============================
\begin{abstract}
This project aims to develop visualizations for the general public of satellite-based flood data from Copernicus Emergency Management Services, focusing on the devastating floods that impacted Pakistan in September 2022. The goal is to effectively communicate the extent, likelihood, and uncertainty of time-varying flood events to non-expert audiences. Methods include a literature review, visualization design, and user evaluation via an online survey, with an emphasis on interpretability and accessibility. At its core, the study compares baseline standard visualizations against new techniques—such as polygon smoothing and binning—to determine how spatial risk data can be made more intuitive.
\end{abstract}

% ==============================
% 1. INTRODUCTION
% ==============================
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
The Copernicus Emergency Management Services (CEMS) dataset provided for this project consists of satellite-observed, time-dependent flood data. The geographic focus of this study is the region adjacent to the city of Larkana, in Sindh province, Pakistan, located within the Indus river basin. We examine the flooding events from September 2022, which were part of an abnormal series of rains between April and October 2022. This disaster affected more than 2.1 million people, resulting in an estimated 1,729 casualties and 12,867 injuries. 

Because the extent of flooding varies significantly over time, visualizing these dynamic changes in a way that is easily understandable to a wider audience presents a particular challenge. The raw data output is often too technical for laypersons, highlighting the need for highly accessible map representations.

% 2. PROBLEM STATEMENT
% ==============================
\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}
The aim of this project is to develop and propose new visualization techniques that can effectively communicate these time-varying flood events, ensuring that they are accessible to the general public. 

The core research question is: \textbf{How can flood risk be effectively combined and visualized for the general public?}

In the context of this study, \textit{flood risk} is defined as a combination of current and future flood \textbf{likelihood} and \textbf{flood extent}. The \textit{general public} refers to civilians without prior experience in analyzing flood visualizations or formal training in geography, flood monitoring, or data science. These individuals must be able to accurately interpret information regarding flood risks even in unfamiliar regions. 

\subsection{Raw Satellite Data Characteristics}
The raw flood data retrieved from Sentinel-1 Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) satellites presents significant challenges for non-expert interpretation. Figure~\ref{fig:raw_likelihood} illustrates the unprocessed likelihood data as provided by the CEMS Global Flood Monitoring system. The visualization displays pixel-level probability values ranging from 0 to 100, where higher values indicate greater confidence that a given area is flooded. Additionally, the dataset includes binary flood extent classifications (flooded vs. not-flooded) derived from the same models, which face similar interpretability challenges for layperson audiences.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{img/baseline_likelihood_raw.png}
    \caption{Raw flood likelihood data retrieved from Sentinel-1 SAR observations for the Pakistan region (September 2022). The blue color intensity represents the probabilistic confidence of flood detection at each pixel, with brighter blue areas indicating higher likelihood of flooding. The granular, noisy appearance is characteristic of unprocessed satellite radar data.}
    \label{fig:raw_likelihood}
\end{figure}

Several characteristics make this raw data difficult for the general public to interpret:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High spatial granularity:} Individual pixels create a fragmented visual appearance that obscures broader flood patterns.
    \item \textbf{Continuous probability scale:} The 0--100 likelihood range requires viewers to mentally categorize risk levels rather than perceiving discrete danger zones.
    \item \textbf{Lack of geographic context:} Without background maps or landmarks, users cannot relate the data to familiar locations or safe routes.
    \item \textbf{Noise and uncertainty:} SAR data inherently contains measurement artifacts and classification uncertainties that appear as visual clutter.
\end{itemize}

These limitations motivate the need for enhanced visualization techniques that preserve critical flood information while significantly improving accessibility and interpretability for non-specialist audiences.

% 3. METHODOLOGY

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Literature Research}
To establish a foundation for effective flood visualizations, existing literature on geospatial data representation and interactive mapping tools was reviewed. Rahman and Thakur (2018) propose overlaying flood maps from different timestamps to highlight temporal changes in flood extent, allowing the persistence of flooding to be visualized. A survey by Yussof et al. (2015) emphasizes that utilizing visualization techniques—including technical, interactive, and 3D approaches—can significantly improve user engagement with complex disaster data. 

The challenge of communicating uncertainty in predictions is addressed by Leedal et al. (2010) and Seipel and Lim (2017), who emphasize the importance of visual encodings (like sequential color scales) that convey confidence levels without overwhelming the viewer. Furthermore, recent studies such as Zhu et al. (2025) note that dynamic or simulated views can increase risk cognition for the general public.

\subsection{Data \& Pre-processing}
\label{subsec:data}
This project utilizes the Global Flood Monitoring (GFM) dataset from CEMS, offering flood detection data based on satellite observations for the selected regions in Pakistan. The dataset provides a comprehensive look at flood risk by supplying four distinct versions of flood likelihood and flood extent generated by different models: \texttt{tuw}, \texttt{dlr}, \texttt{lst}, and an \texttt{ensemble} model. 

In addition to the raw model outputs, the dataset includes crucial masking layers necessary for data refinement:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reference Water Mask:} A flag used to identify permanent inland water bodies (such as existing rivers or lakes).
    \item \textbf{Exclusion Mask:} A flag that identifies and excludes areas of the map that were not successfully scanned by the Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) satellite.
\end{itemize}

During pre-processing, a pixel was only classified as a valid flooded area if the model output indicated flooding, and it was simultaneously not part of the \texttt{exclusion\_mask} and not flagged by the \texttt{reference\_water\_mask}. Furthermore, spatial dimensions were re-projected to standard longitude and latitude (WGS84) to allow compatibility with geographic basemaps.

\subsection{Comparison of Flood Models}
\label{subsec:model_comparison}
To provide a thorough foundation for the visualization techniques, an initial comparison of the four retrieved model types (\texttt{tuw}, \texttt{dlr}, \texttt{lst}, and \texttt{ensemble}) was conducted. Because each model uses slightly different algorithms and radar interpretations to classify water, their resulting likelihoods and extents vary. Understanding these differences is vital before deciding which base data should be simplified for the general public.

The Copernicus Global Flood Monitoring (GFM) dataset derives these models using distinct algorithmic strategies \cite{Wagner2026}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DLR (Single-image classifier):} Uses an unsupervised thresholding procedure on a single SAR image to estimate the \textit{total water extent}, encompassing flooded areas alongside permanent and seasonal water bodies.
    \item \textbf{LST (Dual-image classifier):} Compares the incoming flood image with a recent reference image from the exact same satellite orbit to detect backscatter changes, primarily targeting \textit{recently flooded areas}.
    \item \textbf{TUW (Time-series classifier):} Utilizes a harmonic backscatter model trained on historic observations and Bayesian inference to extract the \textit{flood area compared to a long-term seasonal mean}.
    \item \textbf{Ensemble:} Merges the results of the three independent algorithms at the pixel level. It determines the binary flood extent via a decision-tree-based majority vote and calculates the flood likelihood as the arithmetic mean of the individual likelihoods, maximizing overall robustness.
\end{itemize}

It was decided to use the \texttt{ensemble} model for the final visualizations, as it provides a more balanced and comprehensive representation of flood risk by integrating multiple algorithmic perspectives. For each point on the map Flood Extent is defined as a binary classification of whether the area is flooded or not, while Flood Likelihood is a continuous value between 0 and 100 representing the confidence level of flooding at that location.

\subsection{Visualizations} 
\label{subsec:visualizations}

%% Baseline CEMS
To compare interpretability among laypersons, several visualization techniques were implemented. 
As a baseline, the raw raster Flood Extent data was plotted directly (see Figure \ref{fig:ensemble_baseline}). This approach, while straightforward, resulted in a highly fragmented and noisy visual representation that was difficult for non-experts to interpret. 

The pixel-level granularity obscured broader spatial patterns, making it challenging to identify contiguous flooded areas or understand the overall risk landscape.
Using Likelihood values instead of binary Extent classifications would provided more information but would not significantly improve interpretability due to the continuous nature of the data and lack of clear visual cues for risk levels.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{img/ensemble_flood_extent_on_map.png}
    \caption{ Baseline Flood visualisation with map}
    \label{fig:baseline_with_map}
\end{figure}

%% Baseline with map
In next approach Flood Extent was visualised on map adding spatial context (see Figure \ref{fig:baseline_with_map}). This provided a clearer picture of where flooding was occurring in relation to recognizable geographic features, making it easier for users to understand the spatial distribution of flood risk. 
However, the underlying fragmentation of the data still posed challenges for quickly grasping the overall situation. In this variant risk levels were not visually encoded, so users had to mentally interpret the binary flooded/not-flooded classification without any indication of confidence or severity.

%% Polygon simplified
In the next approach, polygon-based visualizations were introduced to smooth the underlying flood extent values (see Figure \ref{fig:ensemble_flood_extent_polygon_map}). Rather than displaying pixel-level variation, 
the data was aggregated into larger, contiguous polygon regions. The polygons were intentionally generated to be broader and more connected, yielding a smaller number of distinct areas that could be interpreted quickly 
and easily. This smoothing reduces visual fragmentation, creates more uniform spatial structures, and makes overall patterns easier to recognize. The more
clearly defined polygon boundaries also support a better visual estimation of the extent of flooded areas.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{img/ensemble_flood_extent_polygon_map.png}
    \caption{ Polygon-based visualization of flood extent overlaid on a background map.}
    \label{fig:ensemble_flood_extent_polygon_map}
\end{figure}

%% Mixed method
In  the next approach, we wanted to include flood extent and its severity. For this purpose the product of likelihood and flood extent was used to create a new variable called "mixed". 
Overlayed on map this visualization provides a more comprehensive picture of the flood situation, allowing users to see not only where flooding is occurring but also how severe it is in different areas. The color intensity can be used to visually encode the combined risk level, making it easier for users to quickly identify high-risk zones.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{img/ensemble_mixed_on_map.png}
    \caption{Mixed visualization combining flood extent and likelihood, overlaid on a background map.}
    \label{fig:ensemble_mixed_on_map}
\end{figure}

%% Binning  method
Although the mixed visualization contains richer information, its continuous scale remains difficult to interpret quickly for non-experts. To improve readability, a binning step is introduced so that continuous risk values are transformed into a small number of discrete severity classes. Let $x \in [0,100]$ denote the mixed value at a map pixel and let thresholds be defined as
$$
0 \leq v_0 < v_1 < \dots < v_n \leq 100, \qquad v_i \in \mathbb{N}.
$$
Then each pixel is assigned to a class index $c(x)$ by
$$
c(x)=
\begin{cases}
0, & x < v_0,\\
b, & v_b \le x < v_{b+1}, \quad b=0,\dots,n-1,\\
n, & x \ge v_n.
\end{cases}
$$
In implementation, this is performed with threshold-based digitization (equivalent to applying an ordered bin function over all pixels), producing integer classes that are mapped to an ordered colour palette from low to high severity. The key purpose is not to remove information, but to present risk in cognitively simpler categories that support faster comparison between locations and easier communication to the general public.

On the other hand, for smaller scale maps (like the one of Larcana city; see Figure \ref{fig:larcana_binned_polygon}) the polygon smoothing remains beneficial as it reduces visual noise and creates clearer, more cohesive areas of risk that are easier to interpret at a glance.

\section{Survey Structure}
\label{sec:survey}
The survey consisted of three parts and was conducted using SoSci Survey~\cite{SoSciSurvey}, an online platform that supports both survey design and hosting. The target was 30 completed responses, and this target was reached.

The intended audience was deliberately broad and representative of the general public. 
To avoid bias toward expert interpretation, the participant pool was not intended to consist primarily of data science students, 
who typically have above-average familiarity with geospatial visualizations.

The survey was structured as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Part 1 --- Background and Prior Familiarity:} The first page collected basic participant information and prior exposure to the topic. 
    This baseline helps contextualize later task performance and self-reported confidence. Participants answered the question: 
    \emph{How familiar are you with reading flood maps or geospatial maps?} with the options \emph{Not familiar at all}, \emph{Slightly familiar}, 
    \emph{Moderately familiar}, \emph{Very familiar}, and \emph{Expert}.
    \item \textbf{Part 2 --- Visualization Task and Confidence:} This was the core evaluation section. 
    Participants viewed several visualization pages, with page order randomized so no visualization benefited from always being shown first or later. 
    On each page, participants first answered a task question and then rated how confident they were in that answer. 
    The task question was: \emph{Based on this visualization, is it currently safe for a person entering from the south to travel to the yellow point shown on the map?} 
    with answer options \emph{Yes}, \emph{No}, and \emph{Unsure}. The yellow point was intentionally placed so that the theoretically correct answer was \emph{No}, 
    enabling assessment of whether participants interpreted the visualization correctly.
    \item \textbf{Part 3 --- Final Ranking:} The final page asked participants: \emph{Which visualization was the easiest for you to read and understand?} This page was always shown at the end so that each participant had already seen all visualizations at least once. All visualization types were displayed again as references, and participants ranked them by dragging and dropping their names on a scale from \emph{easiest} (left) to \emph{hardest} (right). SoSci Survey returned values from 0 to 1000 for each visualization position, which were then converted into an ordered ranking.
\end{itemize}

An example of a task page is provided in Figure \ref{fig:survey_task_example}:
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{img/questionaire5.png}
    \caption{Example of a survey page from Part 2, showing a visualization with the task question and confidence rating options.}
    \label{fig:survey_task_example}
\end{figure}

Despite careful survey design, several limitations should be acknowledged. 
First, the task question in Part 2 did not differ strongly enough across visualizations. 
After the first few pages, participants may have anticipated the expected answer and repeated it, reducing the discriminative value of later responses. 
Second, the survey was designed and tested on a large computer screen, while many participants likely completed it on mobile devices because distribution occurred via WhatsApp.
This likely reduced map readability and interpretability due to smaller display size. 
The strongest impact was in the final ranking task: on mobile, participants often had to scroll up to re-check visualization examples and then scroll down again to perform drag-and-drop ranking. 
During this process, previously placed items could be reset unintentionally, which may have encouraged approximate ranking from memory rather than careful side-by-side comparison.

\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

The results below are based on the 30 completed interviews (from 53 total responses).

\subsection{Part 1: Prior Familiarity}
Participants largely represented a non-expert audience, which matches the goal of evaluating communication quality for the general public. Most respondents reported low prior familiarity with flood maps: 63.3\% \emph{Not familiar}, 20.0\% \emph{Slightly familiar}, and 16.7\% \emph{Moderately familiar}, with no \emph{Very familiar} or \emph{Expert} responses.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{img/part1_familiarity_distribution.png}
    \caption{Part 1: Self-reported familiarity with reading flood or geospatial maps (n=30 completed responses).}
    \label{fig:part1_familiarity}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Part 2: Task Performance and Confidence}
For the task question (``Is it currently safe to travel to the yellow point?''), the correct answer was \emph{No}. Correct-response rates were relatively close across visualizations: 56.7\% for \emph{Binned}, \emph{Baseline}, and \emph{Baseline with Map}; 50.0\% for \emph{Polygon-based}; and 46.7\% for \emph{Mixed}. Overall, these differences were small and not statistically significant.

Confidence ratings (1--5 scale) were also close: \emph{Polygon-based} had the highest mean confidence (2.83), followed by \emph{Binned} and \emph{Baseline with Map} (both 2.70), while \emph{Baseline} was lowest (2.37). Again, differences were not statistically significant.

\subsection{Interpretation}
Across all three survey parts, differences between visualization variants were visible in tendency but statistically weak. This aligns with the earlier limitations: the Part 2 task may have been too similar across pages, and mobile usage likely reduced reading quality. Still, two practical patterns appear consistently: (1) polygon-based variants were not worse than baseline for lay users and were often ranked slightly better; (2) adding a map background to the basic likelihood view did not produce a clear benefit.



\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This project investigated how flood risk can be visualized for non-expert audiences by comparing basic raster views against polygon-based and binned alternatives. Based on the completed survey sample ($n=30$), all tested visualizations achieved similar task performance, and the differences between methods were not statistically significant.

Nevertheless, the overall pattern suggests that \emph{Polygon-based} and \emph{Binned} offer the best practical balance for public communication: they were among the best-ranked in readability while maintaining similar correctness to the basic representations. In contrast, \emph{Baseline with Map} did not improve interpretation outcomes in this survey.

Overall, the results indicate that simplifying flood-risk structure through polygonal abstraction (with optional binning for discrete risk classes) is a promising direction for communicating dynamic flood information to the general public. Future work should use a larger sample, device-controlled survey setup, and more discriminative task scenarios to better isolate small but meaningful visualization effects.
